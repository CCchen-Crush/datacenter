## Abstract

[Single paragraph, ~250 words, filled from `paper/numbers.md`.] AI data centers are increasingly asked to curtail load several times within one evening peak. Each curtailment leaves two recovery debts behind — deferred computing work with deadlines and depleted energy storage — and both must be repaid through the same limited grid headroom before the next event. This paper establishes a cross-event heterogeneous recovery model of an AI data-center integrated energy system and defines the firm consecutive demand-response capacity as the largest uniform commitment that is delivered in full in every event with a prescribed probability. A recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework is proposed that couples a closed-loop, reliability-constrained day-ahead commitment with an intraday cross-event joint recovery model predictive controller. The results provide a quantitative basis for data centers to offer, and for grid operators to accept, firm consecutive demand-response capacity.

## 1. Introduction

Electricity demand of data centers is growing faster than any other end use. The International Energy Agency projects global data-center consumption to rise from about 415 TWh in 2024 to about 945 TWh by 2030 [1], and in the United States data centers already account for 4.4 % of electricity use [2]. Connection capacity has become the binding constraint for new AI campuses, and flexibility is increasingly treated as a condition for faster interconnection: Norris et al. estimate that curtailing large loads for 0.25–1 % of the year would accommodate 76–126 GW of new load in 22 US balancing areas [6]. AI data centers (AIDCs) are now operated as grid-interactive assets in practice. A field demonstration in Phoenix reduced the power of a production GPU cluster by 25 % for three hours without violating service levels [7]; in the United Kingdom, National Grid, Emerald AI and NVIDIA ran more than 200 simulated grid events in five days. Demand-response (DR) programs reward exactly this behaviour. In Jiangsu, China, the 2024 implementation rules allow up to two agreed DR events per day and additional rapid peak-shaving calls with a short notice, with settlement based on the average reduction against a five-day baseline [5]. Consecutive events within one evening peak are therefore the normal operating condition of a DR-participating AIDC, not an exception.

Each event leaves recovery debts behind. Deferred training and batch work accumulates as a backlog that must be completed before its service deadline, and storage that covered part of the reduction must be recharged. Both debts are repaid by drawing power above the baseline, through the same grid headroom, before the next event starts. Whether an AIDC can deliver the next commitment therefore depends on how it recovered from the previous one. This cross-event dependence determines how much flexibility the grid can actually count on.

**Data-center demand response and computing flexibility.** Workload shifting and local generation for data-center DR were introduced by Liu et al. [9] and reviewed in [10,11]. Recent work quantifies the flexibility of AI workloads: Acun et al. report 18–55 % power flexibility of AI data centers [29], Du et al. add inference quantization as a DR lever [31], Chen and Zheng show that deferring workloads reduces interconnection cost with diminishing returns in the delay window [30], and Williams et al. frame power-flexible AI data centers as grid-responsive compute [28]. In *Applied Energy*, data-center load models for DR [38], the DR potential of liquid-cooled data centers [39] and a four-level joint dispatch of data-center DR [12] have been developed. Overall, existing studies evaluate the curtailment of a single event, and the recovery state that one event passes on to the next remains insufficiently explored.

**Computing–energy coupling in data-center integrated energy systems.** Data centers increasingly operate as integrated energy systems (IES) with combined cooling, heating and power, cold storage and batteries [14,15]. Zhang et al. coupled computing and electricity in a bi-level capacity planning model of a data-center cluster under supply–demand uncertainty [40], Lu et al. studied battery-assisted operation of hyperscale AI data centers [41], and the reliability impact of energy-system flexibility on data centers has been quantified in [42]. These studies capture the coupling between computing, power and heat within a scheduling period. They treat the computing backlog and the state of charge as internal operating states, not as debts that are inherited across DR events and that determine the next deliverable reduction.

**Deliverability and recovery of flexible resources.** Aggregate flexibility models describe the power–energy envelope of homogeneous devices [23], and Evans et al. derived recovery guarantees for aggregated energy storage that must be restored between service requests [33]. Li distinguishes eligible AI workloads from dependable load relief [32], and the scenario approach provides probabilistic guarantees for closed-loop constraint satisfaction [22]. Recovery guarantees, however, have been developed for one type of resource with one type of debt. An AIDC holds two heterogeneous debts at once: computing debt is lossless but deadline-bound, storage debt is lossy but has no deadline, and both compete for the same recovery channel.

To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examined how the heterogeneous recovery demands left by one DR event limit the deliverable capacity of the next one under a shared grid connection, or how this effect can be built into multi-event DR commitments. As a result, the capacity that an AIDC can firmly offer for consecutive events, and the conditions under which coordinated recovery enlarges it, are unknown. To address these gaps, this paper develops a recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework for consecutive DR of an AIDC integrated energy system. The main contributions are as follows.

1. **A cross-event heterogeneous recovery model.** Deadline-bound computing backlogs of three workload classes and lossy storage deficits are modelled as recovery debts that share one grid recovery channel, together with the electric–cooling–heat balances of the AIDC-IES and the Jiangsu settlement rules. Two propositions bound the deliverable capability of a subsequent event and give the closed-form minimum recovery interval and the conditions under which joint recovery is (and is not) valuable.
2. **A firm consecutive DR capacity metric.** The firm capacity $C^{firm}_\rho$ is defined as the largest uniform commitment delivered in full in every event with probability $\rho$, together with its multi-event feasible region. Because scenario feasibility is monotone in the commitment, the chance-constrained capacity is obtained by sample-average evaluation on a commitment grid with block-bootstrap confidence intervals.
3. **A recovery-aware commitment–scheduling framework with a comprehensive validation.** A day-ahead commitment that back-tests the closed-loop controller on historical scenarios under a reliability target is coupled with an intraday cross-event joint recovery MPC; a perfect-recourse stochastic MILP shows why physical-capability assessments overstate firm capacity. The framework is validated against tuned independent recovery, price-coordinated independent recovery and event-by-event joint recovery under fixed and endogenous commitments, three event scenarios, 26 operating days, a four-parameter sensitivity study and a controlled ablation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the system and the consecutive DR problem. Section 3 develops the cross-event recovery model and the firm capacity. Section 4 presents the recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework. Section 5 reports the case studies and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. System description and problem formulation

### 2.1 AI data-center integrated energy system

The AIDC (Fig. 1a) hosts 16 000 H100-class GPUs in 2 000 eight-GPU nodes. Its integrated energy system comprises the grid connection with limit $P^{cap}$, 8 MWp of PV, a 3 MW gas engine whose recovered heat drives a single-effect absorption chiller (CCHP), electric chillers whose COP depends on the ambient temperature, a 10 MWh chilled-water TES and a 6 MW/12 MWh BESS. The GPU load consists of online inference and non-deferrable training (Flex 0) and three classes of deferrable training and batch work (C1–C3) that tolerate a delay up to their lag bound $L_c$ = 0.5, 1.5 and 3 h, corresponding to the flexibility tiers used in industrial AI-DR programs [7].

### 2.2 Consecutive demand response and recovery debts

A DR operator confirms events $k\in\mathcal K$ with commitments $C_k$. Under the Jiangsu rules [5] an event is valid when the average reduction against the five-day baseline reaches 80 % of the commitment; payment is proportional to the reduction up to 120 % of the commitment and a shortfall is assessed at $\kappa$ times the event price. Three event scenarios are studied (Table 2): **S1**, two agreed one-hour events at 17:00 and 19:00 (the rule-compliant base case); **S2**, the same two events with the interval shortened to 30 min; and **S3**, a high-stress extension with a third, rapid peak-shaving event at 21:00 announced 3 h ahead.

During event $k$ the AIDC defers class-$c$ work and discharges storage. At the end of the event it holds the recovery state $\mathbf x_{e_k}=(B_{1},B_{2},B_{3},E,S)$: the backlogs are deadline-bound computing debts, and the deficits $\bar E-E$ and $\bar S-S$ are lossy storage debts. Both are repaid between events by importing power above the baseline, so both compete for the recovery channel
$$H_k=\sum_{t=e_k+1}^{s_{k+1}-1}\big(P^{cap}-P^{base}_t\big)^+\Delta t. \tag{1}$$
The research question is how the recovery state left by event $k$ limits the reduction deliverable in event $k+1$, and how much firm consecutive capacity the AIDC can commit when recovery is coordinated across events.

## 3. Cross-event recovery modelling and deliverable flexibility

### 3.1 Computing side: IT power and backlog

The IT power is
$$P^{IT}_t=P^{fix}+p^{dyn}\Big(\kappa^{inf}G^{inf}_t+G^{fix}_t+\sum_{c}y_{c,t}\Big), \tag{2}$$
with $P^{fix}=Np^{idle}+P^{oth}$. Deferred work of class $c$ accumulates in the backlog
$$B_{c,t}=B_{c,t-1}+\big(a_{c,t}-y_{c,t}\big)\Delta t,\qquad 0\le B_{c,t}\le \bar B_{c,t}(L_c)+v_{c,t}, \tag{3}$$
where $\bar B_{c,t}(L_c)$ is the work that arrived within the last $L_c$ hours and is therefore not yet due, and $v_{c,t}\ge 0$ is overdue work priced at $c^{late}$. The schedulable GPUs are limited by
$$\textstyle\sum_c y_{c,t}\le u^{max}N-G^{inf}_t-G^{fix}_t. \tag{4}$$
Each GPU·h of backlog requires $\varepsilon_t=(1+a+\theta/\mathrm{COP}_t)\,p^{dyn}$ MWh of facility energy when it is caught up, so the computing debt $X_t=\varepsilon_t\sum_cB_{c,t}$ behaves like the discharge of a lossless virtual storage whose recharge has deadlines.

### 3.2 Storage side and integrated energy system

The BESS and TES evolve as
$$E_t=E_{t-1}+\Big(\eta_cP^{ch}_t-\frac{P^{dis}_t}{\eta_d}\Big)\Delta t,\quad \underline E\le E_t\le\bar E,\quad 0\le P^{ch}_t,P^{dis}_t\le\bar P^{B}, \tag{5}$$
$$S_t=(1-\sigma\Delta t)S_{t-1}+\Big(\eta^T_cQ^{TC}_t-\frac{Q^{TD}_t}{\eta^T_d}\Big)\Delta t,\quad \underline S\le S_t\le\bar S. \tag{6}$$
The cooling and electric balances are
$$Q^{EC}_t+\mathrm{COP}^{AC}H^{AC}_t+Q^{TD}_t-Q^{TC}_t=\theta P^{IT}_t+Q_0,\qquad H^{AC}_t\le k^hP^{GT}_t, \tag{7}$$
$$P^{grid}_t+P^{PV}_t+P^{GT}_t+P^{dis}_t-P^{ch}_t=(1+a)P^{IT}_t+P^{aux}+\frac{Q^{EC}_t}{\mathrm{COP}_t}, \tag{8}$$
$$P^{min}\le P^{grid}_t\le P^{cap}, \tag{9}$$
with gas-engine ramp limits and PV curtailment $0\le P^{PV}_t\le\bar P^{PV}_t$.

### 3.3 Settlement of consecutive events

The average reduction of event $k$ is $\bar r_k=\frac{1}{D_k}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_k}(P^{base}_t-P^{grid}_t)\Delta t$. With the validity indicator $z_k\in\{0,1\}$, the paid reduction $R_k$ and the shortfall $s_k$,
$$\bar r_k\ge z_k\,(0.8C_k+\delta)-M(1-z_k),\qquad R_k\le 1.2C_kz_k,\qquad R_k\le\bar r_k+M(1-z_k),\qquad s_k\ge C_k-\bar r_k, \tag{10}$$
where $\delta$ is a planning margin above the 80 % threshold and the big-M terms deactivate the payment constraints of an invalid event. The rule that the load during an event may not exceed the baseline maximum is enforced as a soft constraint. A post-event rebound assessment is not part of the Jiangsu rules; it is treated as a policy parameter in the supplementary material.

### 3.4 Objective

The operator minimizes the comprehensive cost
$$J=\sum_t\Big[\pi_tP^{grid}_t+\frac{c^{gas}}{\eta^{GT}}P^{GT}_t+c^{deg}(P^{ch}_t+P^{dis}_t)+c^{jct}\sum_cB_{c,t}+c^{late}\sum_cv_{c,t}\Big]\Delta t-\sum_k\pi_kD_kR_k+\sum_k\kappa\pi_kD_ks_k+J^{term}, \tag{11}$$
i.e. electricity, gas, storage degradation, delay of deferred work, shortfall assessment minus DR revenue. The terminal term $J^{term}$ values the backlog left at the end of the window at its energy cost and the storage deficit at its recharge cost, so that no strategy can move cost outside the evaluation window.

### 3.5 Firm consecutive DR capacity

Let $\omega\in\Omega$ denote a realisation of computing arrivals, inference load, weather and event calls, and $\bar r_{k,\omega}(C)$ the reduction delivered in event $k$ by a given operating strategy when the uniform commitment $C$ is in force. The **firm consecutive DR capacity** at reliability $\rho$ is
$$C^{firm}_\rho=\max\Big\{C:\ \Pr_\omega\big[\bar r_{k,\omega}(C)\ge (1-\epsilon)C,\ \forall k\in\mathcal K\big]\ge\rho\Big\}, \tag{12}$$
with a metering tolerance $\epsilon$ = 2 %. Its multi-event generalisation is the reliability region $\mathcal C_\rho=\{(C_1,\dots,C_K):\Pr[\bar r_{k,\omega}\ge(1-\epsilon)C_k\ \forall k]\ge\rho\}$, which shows how a higher commitment in one event compresses the deliverable space of the next. $C^{firm}_\rho$ is a deliverability metric for the grid and is reported separately from the settlement validity ($\bar r_k\ge 0.8C_k$).

Because a larger commitment can only tighten the delivery requirement, the delivery indicator is monotone in $C$ for a fixed realisation, and (12) is evaluated by sample average approximation: for every $C$ on a grid $\mathcal G$, the strategy is simulated in closed loop on the realisations $\omega\in\Omega$, and
$$\hat C^{firm}_\rho=\max\Big\{C\in\mathcal G:\ \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\omega}\mathbb 1\big[\bar r_{k,\omega}(C')\ge(1-\epsilon)C'\ \forall k\big]\ge\rho\ \ \forall C'\le C,\ C'\in\mathcal G\Big\}. \tag{13}$$
Confidence intervals of $\hat C^{firm}_\rho$ and of the capacity gain $\Delta C_\rho=C^{firm}_{\rho,JR}-C^{firm}_{\rho,IR}$ are obtained by a block bootstrap over operating days. The relative gain is $I_\rho=\Delta C_\rho/C^{firm}_{\rho,IR}$.

### 3.6 Theoretical properties

**Proposition 1 (capability bound of the next event).** Let event A leave the computing debt $X_A$ and the storage state $E_s$, and let $H$ be the recovery channel before event B of duration $D_B$. The reduction deliverable in B satisfies $\Gamma_B\le\min\{\Gamma^0_B,\Gamma^E,\Gamma^P\}$, where $\Gamma^0_B$ is the single-event capability, the energy bound is $\Gamma^E=(\bar X_B-X_A+E_s+H^{eff})/D_B+g$ with $H^{eff}=H$ if $H\le X_A$ and $X_A+\eta(H-X_A)$ otherwise, and the power bound $\Gamma^P$ accounts for the backlog of A that falls due within B and for work that can be swapped past the end of B. The channel width, the task deadlines and the stored energy therefore jointly determine the next deliverable reduction.

**Proposition 2 (value of joint recovery).** In a single-class model with constant arrivals and a constant channel width $h$, joint recovery repays only the computing debt $c^*=[X-p_f(L-D_B)]^+$ that would fall due before the end of the next event and uses the rest of the channel for recharge, whereas independent recovery repays the full debt $X$ first. The minimum full-recovery intervals differ by
$$\tau^*_{IR}-\tau^*_{JR}=\frac{\min\{X,\ p_f(L-D_B)\}}{h}. \tag{14}$$
Joint recovery has no value when the deadline is not longer than the next event ($L\le D_B$), when storage does not need recharging, or when the channel is either too narrow or wide enough to repay both debts within the interval.

The proofs are given in Appendix B. Proposition 2 explains the mechanism examined in Section 5.3: long-deadline work need not be caught up immediately, which frees the recovery channel for recharging the storage that the next event needs.

## 4. Recovery-aware coordinated scheduling

### 4.1 Day-ahead recovery-aware commitment

The commitment of every event must anticipate the recovery state that the preceding events will leave behind, as delivered by the controller that will actually operate the AIDC. The day-ahead layer therefore evaluates each candidate commitment by closed-loop scenario back-testing. For every $C$ on the grid $\mathcal G$, the intraday controller of Section 4.2 is simulated on the day-ahead scenarios $\omega\in\Omega^{DA}$ (historical operating days), which yields the expected comprehensive cost $\bar J(C)$ and the full-delivery share $\hat p(C)$, and
$$C^*=\arg\min_{C\in\mathcal G}\ \bar J(C)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \hat p(C')\ge\rho\ \ \forall C'\le C, \tag{15}$$
the sample-average counterpart of (12). Every strategy can be paired with this procedure, which makes the endogenous-commitment comparison of Section 5.4 fair: all strategies use the same scenarios and the same objective.

For comparison, the commitments can also be obtained from a two-stage stochastic MILP with perfect recourse, in which $\mathbf C$ is the first-stage decision and the joint recovery model (2)–(11) of every scenario is the second stage,
$$\min_{\mathbf C}\ -\epsilon^{C}\textstyle\sum_kC_k+\sum_{\omega}p_\omega J_\omega(\mathbf C),\qquad \bar r_{k,\omega}\ge(1-\epsilon)C_k-M(1-u_\omega)\ \forall k,\quad \sum_\omega p_\omega u_\omega\ge\rho. \tag{16}$$
Because the recourse of (16) knows each scenario in advance, it measures the physical consecutive capability. In the case study it returns the single-event capabilities (8.18/7.98 MW in S1), so it overestimates the firm consecutive capacity that a real controller delivers. This is why the commitment is determined by (15).

### 4.2 Intraday cross-event joint recovery MPC

Every 15 min the controller solves (2)–(11) over an 18-h horizon with the measured state, forecasts corrected by the latest observed errors and the terminal state references of the day-ahead plan, and applies the first-step decisions (JR-D). Because all confirmed events are visible within the horizon, the controller decides at every step which debt to repay first: it carries long-deadline backlog across events when doing so frees the channel for the storage the next event needs, and it repays the debts that would otherwise fall due. The scenario variant JR-S replaces the forecast by a scenario tree with non-anticipative first-step decisions, in which an event that is not yet confirmed appears only in the scenarios in which it is called. The plant replay enforces the physical limits in the order storage, cold storage and deferrable computing, so that the grid import never exceeds $P^{cap}$.

**Table 1.** Recovery-aware commitment and cross-event joint recovery.

```
Algorithm 1  Recovery-aware commitment and cross-event joint recovery
Input : day-ahead scenarios Ω_DA, test realisations Ω, commitment grid G, reliability ρ,
        event set K, horizon H, plant parameters
Output: commitment C*, firm capacity Ĉ_ρ, closed-loop trajectories
 1  Day-ahead recovery-aware commitment (Eq. (15))
 2    for every C ∈ G: run the closed loop (steps 7-12) on every ω ∈ Ω_DA → expected cost J̄(C), share p̂(C)
 3    C* ← argmin J̄(C) subject to p̂(C') ≥ ρ for all C' ≤ C      (perfect-recourse bound: MILP (16))
 4  Firm capacity on the operating days (SAA, Eq. (13))
 5    for every C ∈ G and ω ∈ Ω: run the closed loop (steps 7-12) with commitment C
 6    Ĉ_ρ ← largest C whose full-delivery share ≥ ρ for all C' ≤ C; bootstrap CIs over days
 7  Intraday operation (every Δt = 15 min)
 8    measure x_t = (B_{1..3,t}, E_t, S_t, gas-engine state), event status and loads
 9    correct the forecasts with the latest observed errors (or build the scenario tree, JR-S)
10    solve the joint recovery MILP (2)-(11) over t … t+H with all confirmed events
11    apply the first-step decisions; replay the plant (storage → TES → compute throttling)
12    update the backlog by earliest-deadline-first execution and the settlement records
13  return C*, Ĉ_ρ and the trajectories
```

### 4.3 Benchmarks

All strategies face the same commitments, service deadlines, physical limits and settlement rules (Table 2).

- **IR-O\*** — independent recovery with an optimised static split. The IT scheduler plans its own share $\alpha C_k$ of every event and its catch-up with priority on the connection headroom; the facility energy-management system then optimises storage and IES with the IT schedule fixed. The split $\alpha$ is tuned day-ahead on the historical scenarios over $\alpha\in[0.2,1.0]$ for every commitment level.
- **IR-P** — price-coordinated independent recovery. Instead of a fixed share, the IT scheduler receives the channel shadow price of the day-ahead joint plan on top of the TOU price (a one-way, day-ahead price signal).
- **JR-M** — event-by-event joint recovery. The joint model is used, but only the current or next event is visible, which represents single-event DR scheduling and isolates the value of cross-event foresight.
- **JR-oracle** — joint recovery with perfect information (reference).

### 4.4 Implementation

The models are implemented in MATLAB/GNU Octave and solved with GLPK (Gurobi and `intlinprog` are supported). The intraday MILP has about $10^4$ variables and one binary per event; a closed-loop day of JR-D takes 4–5 s and of JR-S about 60 s on one CPU core.
