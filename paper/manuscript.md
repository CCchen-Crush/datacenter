## Abstract

AI data centers are increasingly asked to curtail load several times within one evening peak, each time leaving deferred computing work and depleted storage that compete for grid headroom before the next event. This paper models these cross-event recovery debts in an AI data-center integrated energy system, bounds the next event's deliverable reduction and derives the closed-form value of joint recovery. The firm consecutive demand-response capacity is defined as the largest uniform commitment delivered in full in every event with a prescribed probability, and the day-ahead commitment is set from the controller's closed-loop capability on historical days within a Wasserstein ball, solved exactly by sorting. A recovery-aware scheduling framework couples it with intraday cross-event joint recovery by model predictive control. On a 16 000-GPU data center under the Jiangsu demand-response rules, over 26 operating days from public traces, cross-event joint recovery firmly delivers 7.5 MW with two events, 94 % of the physical capability, and 6.5 MW with three events. Tuned and price-coordinated independent recovery is not firm even at 1 MW: baseline deviations fall on storage that serves a fixed share of the reduction. Cross-event foresight adds up to 3.0 MW at a 30-min interval, vanishes ⟨beyond about 2 h⟩ and substitutes ⟨9.3 MWh of battery, about 5.6 million CNY⟩. The cost-optimal robust commitment is delivered in full on every operating day, a perfect-recourse two-stage commitment on only 85 % of them. Joint recovery also yields the largest benefit, 43–45 thousand CNY per day, with the shortest task delays.

**Keywords:** AI data center; consecutive demand response; computing–storage recovery; firm deliverable capacity; integrated energy system; model predictive control

## 1. Introduction

Electricity demand of data centers is growing faster than any other end use. The International Energy Agency projects global data-center consumption to rise from about 415 TWh in 2024 to about 945 TWh by 2030 [1], and in the United States data centers already account for 4.4 % of electricity use [2]. Connection capacity has become the binding constraint for new AI campuses, and flexibility is increasingly treated as a condition for faster interconnection: Norris et al. estimate that curtailing large loads for 0.25–1 % of the year would accommodate 76–126 GW of new load in 22 US balancing areas [6]. AI data centers (AIDCs) are now operated as grid-interactive assets in practice. A field demonstration in Phoenix reduced the power of a production GPU cluster by 25 % for three hours without violating service levels [7]; in the United Kingdom, National Grid, Emerald AI and NVIDIA ran more than 200 simulated grid events in five days. Demand-response (DR) programs reward exactly this behaviour. In Jiangsu, China, the 2024 implementation rules allow up to two agreed DR events per day and additional rapid peak-shaving calls with a short notice, with settlement based on the average reduction against a five-day baseline [5]. Consecutive events within one evening peak are therefore the normal operating condition of a DR-participating AIDC, not an exception.

Each event leaves recovery debts behind. Deferred training and batch work accumulates as a backlog that must be completed before its service deadline, and storage that covered part of the reduction must be recharged. Both debts are repaid by drawing power above the baseline, through the same grid headroom, before the next event starts. Whether an AIDC can deliver the next commitment therefore depends on how it recovered from the previous one. This cross-event dependence determines how much flexibility the grid can actually count on.

**Data-center demand response and computing flexibility.** Workload shifting and local generation for data-center DR were introduced by Liu et al. [9] and reviewed in [10,11]. Recent work quantifies the flexibility of AI workloads: Acun et al. report 18–55 % power flexibility of AI data centers [29], Du et al. add inference quantization as a DR lever [31], Chen and Zheng show that deferring workloads reduces interconnection cost with diminishing returns in the delay window [30], and Williams et al. frame power-flexible AI data centers as grid-responsive compute [28]. In *Applied Energy*, data-center load models for DR [38], the DR potential of liquid-cooled data centers [39] and a four-level joint dispatch of data-center DR [12] have been developed. Overall, existing studies evaluate the curtailment of a single event, and the recovery state that one event passes on to the next remains insufficiently explored.

**Computing–energy coupling in data-center integrated energy systems.** Data centers increasingly operate as integrated energy systems (IES) with combined cooling, heating and power, cold storage and batteries [14,15]. Zhang et al. coupled computing and electricity in a bi-level capacity planning model of a data-center cluster under supply–demand uncertainty [40], Lu et al. studied battery-assisted operation of hyperscale AI data centers [41], and the reliability impact of energy-system flexibility on data centers has been quantified in [42]. These studies capture the coupling between computing, power and heat within a scheduling period. They treat the computing backlog and the state of charge as internal operating states, not as debts that are inherited across DR events and that determine the next deliverable reduction.

**Deliverability and recovery of flexible resources.** Aggregate flexibility models describe the power–energy envelope of homogeneous devices [23], and Evans et al. derived recovery guarantees for aggregated energy storage that must be restored between service requests [33]. Li distinguishes eligible AI workloads from dependable load relief [32], and the scenario approach provides probabilistic guarantees for closed-loop constraint satisfaction [22]. Distributionally robust chance constraints over Wasserstein ambiguity sets give data-driven reliability guarantees [43,45] and are widely used for flexibility commitments; their two-stage form, solved by column-and-constraint generation [46], evaluates the second stage with perfect recourse and therefore certifies physical capability rather than the deliverability of the controller that will operate the resource. Recovery guarantees, moreover, have been developed for one type of resource with one type of debt. An AIDC holds two heterogeneous debts at once: computing debt is lossless but deadline-bound, storage debt is lossy but has no deadline, and both compete for the same recovery channel.

To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examined how the heterogeneous recovery demands left by one DR event limit the deliverable capacity of the next one under a shared grid connection, or how this effect can be built into multi-event DR commitments. As a result, the capacity that an AIDC can firmly offer for consecutive events, and the conditions under which coordinated recovery enlarges it, are unknown. To address these gaps, this paper develops a recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework for consecutive DR of an AIDC integrated energy system. The main contributions are as follows.

1. **A cross-event heterogeneous recovery model.** Deadline-bound computing backlogs of three workload classes and lossy storage deficits are modelled as recovery debts that share one grid recovery channel, together with the electric–cooling–heat balances of the AIDC-IES and the Jiangsu settlement rules. Two propositions bound the deliverable capability of a subsequent event and give the closed-form minimum recovery interval and the conditions under which joint recovery is (and is not) valuable.
2. **Firm consecutive DR capacity and its distributionally robust commitment.** The firm capacity $C^{firm}_\rho$ is defined as the largest uniform commitment delivered in full in every event with probability $\rho$, together with its multi-event reliability region. The commitment is computed from the closed-loop capability of the controller on historical days with a 1-Wasserstein ambiguity set; Proposition 3 gives its exact solution by sorting and shows that it reduces to the sample-average capacity for a zero radius. A perfect-recourse two-stage model, in contrast, overstates the deliverable capacity because it measures physical capability.
3. **A recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework with a system-level validation.** The day-ahead commitment is coupled with an intraday cross-event joint recovery MPC. The framework is validated against tuned and price-coordinated independent recovery and event-by-event joint recovery under fixed and endogenous commitments, three event scenarios and 26 operating days. The validation separates the value of coordination from the value of cross-event foresight, converts the capacity gain into equivalent battery energy and connection capacity, quantifies the contribution of the gas engine, cold storage and absorption chiller, and identifies the conditions under which joint recovery pays off.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the system and the consecutive DR problem. Section 3 develops the cross-event recovery model and the firm capacity. Section 4 presents the recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework. Section 5 reports the case studies and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. System description and problem formulation

### 2.1 AI data-center integrated energy system

The AIDC (Fig. 1a) hosts 16 000 H100-class GPUs in 2 000 eight-GPU nodes. Its integrated energy system comprises the grid connection with limit $P^{cap}$, 8 MWp of PV, a 3 MW gas engine whose recovered heat drives a single-effect absorption chiller (CCHP), electric chillers whose COP depends on the ambient temperature, a 10 MWh chilled-water TES and a 6 MW/12 MWh BESS. The GPU load consists of online inference and non-deferrable training (Flex 0) and three classes of deferrable training and batch work (C1–C3) that tolerate a delay up to their lag bound $L_c$ = 0.5, 1.5 and 3 h, corresponding to the flexibility tiers used in industrial AI-DR programs [7].

### 2.2 Consecutive demand response and recovery debts

A DR operator confirms events $k\in\mathcal K$ with commitments $C_k$. Under the Jiangsu rules [5] an event is valid when the average reduction against the five-day baseline reaches 80 % of the commitment; payment is proportional to the reduction up to 120 % of the commitment and a shortfall is assessed at $\kappa$ times the event price. Three event scenarios are studied (Table 3): **S1**, two agreed one-hour events at 17:00 and 19:00 (the rule-compliant base case); **S2**, the same two events with the interval shortened to 30 min; and **S3**, a high-stress extension with a third, rapid peak-shaving event at 21:00 announced 3 h ahead.

During event $k$ the AIDC defers class-$c$ work and discharges storage. At the end of the event it holds the recovery state $\mathbf x_{e_k}=(B_{1},B_{2},B_{3},E,S)$: the backlogs are deadline-bound computing debts, and the deficits $\bar E-E$ and $\bar S-S$ are lossy storage debts. Both are repaid between events by importing power above the baseline, so both compete for the recovery channel
$$H_k=\sum_{t=e_k+1}^{s_{k+1}-1}\big(P^{cap}-P^{base}_t\big)^+\Delta t. \tag{1}$$
The research question is how the recovery state left by event $k$ limits the reduction deliverable in event $k+1$, and how much firm consecutive capacity the AIDC can commit when recovery is coordinated across events.

## 3. Cross-event recovery modelling and deliverable flexibility

### 3.1 Computing side: IT power and backlog

The IT power is
$$P^{IT}_t=P^{fix}+p^{dyn}\Big(\kappa^{inf}G^{inf}_t+G^{fix}_t+\sum_{c}y_{c,t}\Big), \tag{2}$$
with $P^{fix}=Np^{idle}+P^{oth}$. Deferred work of class $c$ accumulates in the backlog
$$B_{c,t}=B_{c,t-1}+\big(a_{c,t}-y_{c,t}\big)\Delta t,\qquad 0\le B_{c,t}\le \bar B_{c,t}(L_c)+v_{c,t}, \tag{3}$$
where $\bar B_{c,t}(L_c)$ is the work that arrived within the last $L_c$ hours and is therefore not yet due, and $v_{c,t}\ge 0$ is overdue work priced at $c^{late}$. The schedulable GPUs are limited by
$$\textstyle\sum_c y_{c,t}\le u^{max}N-G^{inf}_t-G^{fix}_t. \tag{4}$$
Each GPU·h of backlog requires $\varepsilon_t=(1+a+\theta/\mathrm{COP}_t)\,p^{dyn}$ MWh of facility energy when it is caught up, so the computing debt $X_t=\varepsilon_t\sum_cB_{c,t}$ behaves like the discharge of a lossless virtual storage whose recharge has deadlines.

### 3.2 Storage side and integrated energy system

The BESS and TES evolve as
$$E_t=E_{t-1}+\Big(\eta_cP^{ch}_t-\frac{P^{dis}_t}{\eta_d}\Big)\Delta t,\quad \underline E\le E_t\le\bar E,\quad 0\le P^{ch}_t,P^{dis}_t\le\bar P^{B}, \tag{5}$$
$$S_t=(1-\sigma\Delta t)S_{t-1}+\Big(\eta^T_cQ^{TC}_t-\frac{Q^{TD}_t}{\eta^T_d}\Big)\Delta t,\quad \underline S\le S_t\le\bar S. \tag{6}$$
The cooling and electric balances are
$$Q^{EC}_t+\mathrm{COP}^{AC}H^{AC}_t+Q^{TD}_t-Q^{TC}_t=\theta P^{IT}_t+Q_0,\qquad H^{AC}_t\le k^hP^{GT}_t, \tag{7}$$
$$P^{grid}_t+P^{PV}_t+P^{GT}_t+P^{dis}_t-P^{ch}_t=(1+a)P^{IT}_t+P^{aux}+\frac{Q^{EC}_t}{\mathrm{COP}_t}, \tag{8}$$
$$P^{min}\le P^{grid}_t\le P^{cap}, \tag{9}$$
with gas-engine ramp limits and PV curtailment $0\le P^{PV}_t\le\bar P^{PV}_t$.

### 3.3 Settlement of consecutive events

The average reduction of event $k$ is $\bar r_k=\frac{1}{D_k}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_k}(P^{base}_t-P^{grid}_t)\Delta t$. With the validity indicator $z_k\in\{0,1\}$, the paid reduction $R_k$ and the shortfall $s_k$,
$$\bar r_k\ge z_k\,(0.8C_k+\delta)-M(1-z_k),\qquad R_k\le 1.2C_kz_k,\qquad R_k\le\bar r_k+M(1-z_k),\qquad s_k\ge C_k-\bar r_k, \tag{10}$$
where $\delta$ is a planning margin above the 80 % threshold and the big-M terms deactivate the payment constraints of an invalid event. The rule that the load during an event may not exceed the baseline maximum is enforced as a soft constraint. A post-event rebound assessment is not part of the Jiangsu rules; it is treated as a policy parameter in the supplementary material.

### 3.4 Objective

The operator minimizes the comprehensive cost
$$J=\sum_t\Big[\pi_tP^{grid}_t+\frac{c^{gas}}{\eta^{GT}}P^{GT}_t+c^{deg}(P^{ch}_t+P^{dis}_t)+c^{jct}\sum_cB_{c,t}+c^{late}\sum_cv_{c,t}\Big]\Delta t-\sum_k\pi_kD_kR_k+\sum_k\kappa\pi_kD_ks_k+J^{term}, \tag{11}$$
i.e. electricity, gas, storage degradation, delay of deferred work, shortfall assessment minus DR revenue. The terminal term $J^{term}$ values the backlog left at the end of the window at its energy cost and the storage deficit at its recharge cost, so that no strategy can move cost outside the evaluation window.

### 3.5 Firm consecutive DR capacity

Let $\omega\in\Omega$ denote a realisation of computing arrivals, inference load, weather and event calls, and $\bar r_{k,\omega}(C)$ the reduction delivered in event $k$ by a given operating strategy when the uniform commitment $C$ is in force. The **firm consecutive DR capacity** at reliability $\rho$ is
$$C^{firm}_\rho=\max\Big\{C:\ \Pr_\omega\big[\bar r_{k,\omega}(C)\ge (1-\epsilon_f)C,\ \forall k\in\mathcal K\big]\ge\rho\Big\}, \tag{12}$$
with a metering tolerance $\epsilon_f$ = 2 %. Its multi-event generalisation is the reliability region $\mathcal C_\rho=\{(C_1,\dots,C_K):\Pr[\bar r_{k,\omega}\ge(1-\epsilon_f)C_k\ \forall k]\ge\rho\}$, which shows how a higher commitment in one event compresses the deliverable space of the next. $C^{firm}_\rho$ is a deliverability metric for the grid and is reported separately from the settlement validity ($\bar r_k\ge 0.8C_k$).

Because a larger commitment can only tighten the delivery requirement, the delivery indicator is monotone in $C$ for a fixed realisation, and (12) is evaluated by sample average approximation: for every $C$ on a grid $\mathcal G$, the strategy is simulated in closed loop on the realisations $\omega\in\Omega$, and
$$\hat C^{firm}_\rho=\max\Big\{C\in\mathcal G:\ \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\omega}\mathbb 1\big[\bar r_{k,\omega}(C')\ge(1-\epsilon_f)C'\ \forall k\big]\ge\rho\ \ \forall C'\le C,\ C'\in\mathcal G\Big\}. \tag{13}$$
Confidence intervals of $\hat C^{firm}_\rho$ and of the capacity gain $\Delta C_\rho=C^{firm}_{\rho,JR}-C^{firm}_{\rho,IR}$ are obtained by a block bootstrap over operating days. The relative gain is $I_\rho=\Delta C_\rho/C^{firm}_{\rho,IR}$.

### 3.6 Theoretical properties

**Proposition 1 (capability bound of the next event).** Let event A leave the computing debt $X_A$ and the storage state $E_s$, and let $H$ be the recovery channel before event B of duration $D_B$. The reduction deliverable in B satisfies $\Gamma_B\le\min\{\Gamma^0_B,\Gamma^E,\Gamma^P\}$, where $\Gamma^0_B$ is the single-event capability, the energy bound is $\Gamma^E=(\bar X_B-X_A+E_s+H^{eff})/D_B+g$ with $H^{eff}=H$ if $H\le X_A$ and $X_A+\eta(H-X_A)$ otherwise, and the power bound $\Gamma^P$ accounts for the backlog of A that falls due within B and for work that can be swapped past the end of B. The channel width, the task deadlines and the stored energy therefore jointly determine the next deliverable reduction.

**Proposition 2 (value of joint recovery).** In a single-class model with constant arrivals and a constant channel width $h$, joint recovery repays only the computing debt $c^*=[X-p_f(L-D_B)]^+$ that would fall due before the end of the next event and uses the rest of the channel for recharge, whereas independent recovery repays the full debt $X$ first. The minimum full-recovery intervals differ by
$$\tau^*_{IR}-\tau^*_{JR}=\frac{\min\{X,\ p_f(L-D_B)\}}{h}. \tag{14}$$
Joint recovery has no value when the deadline is not longer than the next event ($L\le D_B$), when storage does not need recharging, or when the channel is either too narrow or wide enough to repay both debts within the interval.

The proofs are given in Appendix B. Proposition 2 explains the mechanism examined in Section 5.2: long-deadline work need not be caught up immediately, which frees the recovery channel for recharging the storage that the next event needs.

## 4. Recovery-aware coordinated scheduling

### 4.1 Day-ahead recovery-aware commitment

The commitment of every event must anticipate the recovery state that the preceding events will leave behind, as delivered by the controller that will actually operate the AIDC. The day-ahead layer therefore measures the capability of that controller in closed loop. For every historical day $d$ of $\Omega^{DA}$ and every candidate commitment $C$ of the grid $\mathcal G$, the intraday controller of Section 4.2 is simulated with the realised data of day $d$, which yields the comprehensive cost $J_d(C)$ and the capability
$$\xi_d=\max\big\{C\in\mathcal G:\ \bar r_{k,d}(C')\ge(1-\epsilon_f)C'\ \ \forall k,\ \forall C'\le C,\ C'\in\mathcal G\big\}+\tfrac12\Delta_{\mathcal G}, \tag{15}$$
the midpoint between the largest uniform commitment that the controller delivers in full on day $d$ and the next commitment of the grid (step $\Delta_{\mathcal G}$), at which delivery first fails. The back-test visits the commitments in increasing order and stops simulating a day after its first failure; this yields every $\xi_d$, and every $J_d(C)$ for $C\le\min_d\xi_d$, with about half of the closed-loop runs of the full grid. The empirical distribution $\hat P_N=\frac1N\sum_d\delta_{\xi_d}$ summarises the deliverability of the controller, but the operating day can differ from the historical days (Section 5.1). The commitment is therefore protected against all distributions within the 1-Wasserstein ball $\mathcal B_\varepsilon(\hat P_N)=\{Q:\ W_1(Q,\hat P_N)\le\varepsilon\}$:
$$C^{DRO}_\rho(\varepsilon)=\max\Big\{C\in\mathcal G:\ \inf_{Q\in\mathcal B_\varepsilon(\hat P_N)}Q(\xi\ge C)\ge\rho\Big\}, \tag{16}$$
the distributionally robust counterpart of (12). The radius $\varepsilon$ (MW) is the largest average shift of the capability that the commitment must withstand.

**Proposition 3 (exact distributionally robust firm capacity).** For a commitment $C$, let $n(C)=|\{d:\xi_d\ge C\}|$, let $\delta_{(1)}(C)\le\delta_{(2)}(C)\le\dots$ be the sorted margins $\xi_d-C$ of these days, and $r(C)=n(C)-\rho N$. The robust constraint in (16) holds if and only if $r(C)\ge0$ and
$$\Phi_\rho(C)=\frac1N\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor r\rfloor}\delta_{(j)}(C)+\big(r-\lfloor r\rfloor\big)\,\delta_{(\lfloor r\rfloor+1)}(C)\Big]\ \ge\ \varepsilon . \tag{17}$$
Hence (i) $C^{DRO}_\rho(\varepsilon)=\max\{C\in\mathcal G:\ r(C)\ge0,\ \Phi_\rho(C)\ge\varepsilon\}$ is obtained by sorting; (ii) for $\varepsilon=0$ it equals the sample-average capacity, the empirical $(1-\rho)$-quantile of $\xi$; (iii) it is non-increasing in $\varepsilon$, and it is insensitive to the radius when the days just above the commitment keep a large margin, i.e. when the capability distribution is concentrated. $\Phi_\rho(C)$ is the smallest average capability loss (MW) that would push the full-delivery probability below $\rho$; it is the certified reliability margin of the commitment. The proof is given in Appendix B.

The radius is set to the expected 1-Wasserstein estimation error of the empirical distribution [44],
$$\varepsilon_N=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\int\sqrt{\hat F_N(\xi)\big(1-\hat F_N(\xi)\big)}\,d\xi, \tag{18a}$$
which is small for a concentrated capability distribution and large for a dispersed one; a leave-one-out validation on $\Omega^{DA}$ serves as a check (Section 5.6). Among the robustly deliverable commitments, the operator chooses the one with the lowest back-tested cost,
$$C^*=\arg\min\big\{\bar J(C)=\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_dJ_d(C):\ C\in\mathcal G,\ C\le C^{DRO}_\rho(\varepsilon)\big\}. \tag{18}$$
Every strategy can be paired with this procedure, which makes the endogenous-commitment comparison of Section 5.6 fair: all strategies use the same days, the same objective and the same reliability target.

For comparison, the commitments can also be obtained from a two-stage stochastic MILP with perfect recourse, in which $\mathbf C$ is the first-stage decision and the joint recovery model (2)–(11) of every scenario is the second stage,
$$\min_{\mathbf C}\ -\epsilon^{C}\textstyle\sum_kC_k+\sum_{\omega}p_\omega J_\omega(\mathbf C),\qquad \bar r_{k,\omega}\ge(1-\epsilon_f)C_k-M(1-u_\omega)\ \forall k,\quad \sum_\omega p_\omega u_\omega\ge\rho. \tag{19}$$
Because the recourse of (19) knows each scenario in advance, it measures the physical consecutive capability. In the case study it returns the single-event capabilities (8.18/7.98 MW in S1) and thus overstates the firm consecutive capacity that a real controller delivers. Two-stage distributionally robust models solved by column-and-constraint generation share this recourse structure; the closed-loop capability (15) avoids it.

### 4.2 Intraday cross-event joint recovery MPC

Every 15 min the controller solves (2)–(11) over an 18-h horizon with the measured state, forecasts corrected by the latest observed errors and the terminal state references of the day-ahead plan, and applies the first-step decisions (JR-D). Because all confirmed events are visible within the horizon, the controller decides at every step which debt to repay first: it carries long-deadline backlog across events when doing so frees the channel for the storage the next event needs, and it repays the debts that would otherwise fall due. The scenario variant JR-S replaces the forecast by a scenario tree with non-anticipative first-step decisions, in which an event that is not yet confirmed appears only in the scenarios in which it is called. The plant replay enforces the physical limits in the order storage, cold storage and deferrable computing, so that the grid import never exceeds $P^{cap}$.

### 4.3 Benchmarks

All strategies face the same commitments, service deadlines, physical limits and settlement rules (Table 2).

- **IR-O\*** — independent recovery with an optimised static split. The IT scheduler plans its own share $\alpha C_k$ of every event and its catch-up with priority on the connection headroom; the facility energy-management system then optimises storage and IES with the IT schedule fixed. The split $\alpha$ is tuned day-ahead on the historical scenarios over $\alpha\in[0.2,1.0]$ for every commitment level.
- **IR-P** — price-coordinated independent recovery. Instead of a fixed share, the IT scheduler receives the channel shadow price of the day-ahead joint plan on top of the TOU price (a one-way, day-ahead price signal).
- **JR-M** — event-by-event joint recovery. The joint model is used, but only the current or next event is visible, which represents single-event DR scheduling and isolates the value of cross-event foresight.
- **JR-oracle** — joint recovery with perfect information (reference).

### 4.4 Implementation

The models are implemented in MATLAB/GNU Octave and solved with GLPK (Gurobi and `intlinprog` are supported). The intraday MILP has about $10^4$ variables and one binary per event; a closed-loop day of JR-D takes 4–5 s and of JR-S about 60 s on one CPU core.

### 5.2 Recovery mechanism on a typical day

Figs. 3, 4 and 11 trace the two events of S2 on a representative operating day with a uniform commitment of 7 MW, and contrast them with the same events 3 h apart. With the 30-min interval, JR-D delivers 8.18 MW in the first and 8.02 MW in the second event, IR-O\* delivers 7.00 MW and 6.84 MW and misses full delivery in the second event, and JR-M collapses to 4.64 MW in the second event (Fig. 3a–b). With the 3-h interval all three strategies deliver both events in full (Fig. 4). On this day the shortfall of IR-O\* is small (2 % of the commitment); on other operating days of the same scenario it reaches 17 %, while the 3-h interval again restores full delivery. The difference therefore arises entirely from how the debts of the first event are incurred and repaid.

**Which debt to incur.** JR-D defers the long-deadline class C3 during the first event: at its end, 3.0×10³ GPU·h of C3 work are deferred, against 2.0×10³ GPU·h under IR-O\* (Fig. 4a). Because C3 does not fall due before the end of the second event, this debt can be carried across it, which is the mechanism of Proposition 2: the necessary catch-up $c^*$ is limited to the work that would fall due in the next event.

**How to use the channel.** In the 30-min interval the grid import of all strategies reaches the connection limit. IR-O\* spends the channel on catch-up and recharges the BESS by only 0.16 MWh, whereas JR-D recharges it by 0.73 MWh, devotes 57 % of the recovery energy drawn in the interval to storage instead of 32 %, and starts the second event with 6.22 MWh instead of 5.91 MWh (Figs. 3c–d, 11). JR-M, which plans each event alone, over-delivers the first event to collect the 120 % payment and empties the BESS to 1.27 MWh; the backlog it leaves cannot be deferred again and the second event fails.

**Who provides the channel.** In the evening the gas engine and the absorption chiller provide 85 % of the energy that recovery draws through the channel (Fig. 11, Fig. 12b): the gas engine runs at full output between 14:00 and 21:30 with and without DR, so it does not add energy during recovery but lowers the baseline import by 3 MW and thereby widens the headroom $P^{cap}-P^{base}$. The cold storage is pre-charged before the events, covers part of the cooling load during both events and is recharged at night, so its debt never enters the recovery channel.

These results confirm that the deliverable reduction of the second event is decided by the recovery state that the first event leaves behind, and that joint recovery shapes this state through both the choice of deferred work and the allocation of the channel.

Fig. 5 shows the share of the 26 operating days on which every event is delivered in full, as a function of the uniform commitment, and Table 5 lists the firm capacities. ### 5.6 Distributionally robust commitment

**Capabilities.** Fig. 10a shows the closed-loop capability $\xi_d$ (15) of every controller on the 14 historical days and on the 26 operating days. Independent recovery has a dispersed, bimodal capability: IR-O\* fails already at 1 MW on 4 of the 14 historical days and reaches 7.75–8.25 MW on the others, and the same pattern recurs on the operating days (8 of 26 below 1 MW). The capability of JR-M lies at 7.75–8.25 MW on the historical days and spreads down to 6.25 MW on the operating days. JR-D reaches 8.25 MW, the midpoint between the last delivered and the first failed commitment at its physical limit, on every historical day and on 21 of the 26 operating days.

**Robust capacity.** With $\rho$ = 0.95 and $N$ = 14, the sample-average capacity is the smallest historical capability rounded down to the grid: below 1 MW for IR-O\* and IR-P, 7.5 MW for JR-M and 8.0 MW for JR-D (Table 4). ### 5.7 Role of the integrated energy system

The integrated energy system provides the recovery channel. In the evening the gas engine and the absorption chiller supply 85 % of the energy that recovery draws through the channel (Figs. 11 and 12b): the gas engine runs at full output from 14:00 to 21:30 with and without DR, so it lowers the baseline import by 3 MW and widens the headroom $P^{cap}-P^{base}$, and the absorption chiller moves part of the cooling load off the electric chillers. The cold storage is pre-charged before the events, covers part of the cooling load during both events and is recharged at night, so its debt never enters the channel.

With the BESS alone (no computing flexibility, no cold storage), no commitment of 1 MW or more is firm; with computing flexibility alone the firm capacity is 3.00 MW; with both, still without the cold storage, it is 7.50 MW, nearly twice what the two resources achieve separately (less than 4 MW together). ### 5.8 Service quality, economics and robustness

**Service quality.** No strategy leaves work overdue: the deadline constraints (3) and earliest-deadline-first execution keep every deferred task within its lag bound on all days and at all commitments. What DR costs the computing users is the extra completion time of the deferred work (Fig. 13). At every commitment JR-D defers work for less time than the other controllers that deliver: at 7 MW the mean extra completion time is 0.68 h in S1 and 0.90 h in S2, against 0.85 h and 1.12 h for IR-O\* and 1.35 h and 1.26 h for JR-M, and JR-D uses 36 % and 46 % of the SLA slack against 60–75 % for IR-O\* and 60–64 % for JR-M. JR-D defers the long-deadline class, which has slack to spare, whereas IR-O\* catches up its own share at once and JR-M over-delivers the first event and must defer more in the second. IR-P defers least (0.52 h in S1) because it delivers least. The firm operating points of JR-D therefore form the capacity–service front in both scenarios: a larger firm commitment does not come at the expense of the computing users.

**Economics.** Fig. 14 compares the daily costs at 7 MW, a commitment that all strategies can attempt and the one they select in the back-test of Section 5.6. JR-D yields the largest benefit relative to no DR participation, 44.8 k CNY/day in S1 and 43.1 k CNY/day in S2. Its DR revenue of 48.3 k CNY reaches 96 % of the payment cap of the two events and is offset mainly by the delay cost of the deferred work (4.4–6.4 k CNY/day); the electricity and gas costs fall slightly because consumption is shifted out of the evening peak. IR-O\* gains 40.3 and 38.5 k CNY/day with a higher delay cost (5.9–7.6 k CNY/day). JR-M gains 37.3 k CNY/day in S1 but only 19.1 k CNY/day in S2: there the second event finds the AIDC with 4.1×10³ GPU·h of backlog and 3.4 MWh of stored energy (JR-D: 2.2×10³ GPU·h and 6.0 MWh), so its revenue drops to 32.2 k CNY and a shortfall assessment of 4.7 k CNY is levied. IR-P gains 17.8 and 29.8 k CNY/day. On a paired basis JR-D is cheaper than every benchmark on all 26 days in both scenarios; its mean advantage is 4.5 k CNY/day over IR-O\* (95 % CI 2.5–7.6) and 7.6 k CNY/day over JR-M (6.4–8.8) in S1, and 4.6 k CNY/day (2.7–7.4) and 24.1 k CNY/day (19.6–28.5) in S2. ## 6. Conclusions

This paper examined how the recovery debts left by one demand-response event limit the next one in an AI data-center integrated energy system, and how much consecutive DR capacity such a data center can firmly commit. A cross-event heterogeneous recovery model, the firm consecutive DR capacity with its distributionally robust commitment, and a recovery-aware coordinated scheduling framework were developed and tested on a 16 000-GPU data center under the Jiangsu rules, with 26 operating days built from public computing traces. The main conclusions are as follows.

1. **Deliverability is decided during recovery.** Computing backlog and storage deficit compete for one recovery channel. Joint recovery carries the long-deadline backlog across the next event and spends the channel on recharging: with a 30-min interval it devotes 57 % of the recovery energy to storage instead of 32 % under independent recovery, and it delivers both 7 MW events in full where independent and event-by-event recovery fall short.
2. **Firm capacity requires joint recovery.** Cross-event joint recovery firmly delivers 7.5 MW in the two-event scenarios, 94 % of the physical capability of the second event, and 6.5 MW with a third, rapid event. Independent recovery, with a day-ahead tuned split or with price coordination, is not firm at any commitment down to 1 MW, because baseline deviations of up to 3.3 MW fall on storage that serves a fixed share of the reduction.
3. **Foresight pays off at short intervals; coordination pays off always.** Cross-event foresight adds 3.0 MW at a 30-min and 1.0 MW at a 1-h interval, and 2.0 MW with three events; it vanishes ⟨beyond about 2 h⟩, as Proposition 2 predicts. ⟨Coordination adds at least … MW at every interval.⟩
4. **Joint recovery substitutes hardware.** ⟨Cross-event foresight saves 9.3 MWh of battery (about 5.6 million CNY) …; connection equivalent …⟩ No battery size makes independent recovery firm, and batteries beyond 12 MWh erode the baseline and lower the firm capacity.
5. **Commit what the controller can deliver.** A closed-loop back-test on 14 historical days refuses to commit independent recovery, which fails at 1 MW on 4 of them, and its Wasserstein-robust version corrects the over-confident commitment of event-by-event recovery (7.5 → 6.0 MW, full delivery out of sample 77 → 100 %). The cost-optimal commitment of cross-event joint recovery, 7 MW, is delivered on every operating day, whereas a perfect-recourse two-stage model commits the single-event capabilities (8.18/7.98 MW), which are delivered in full on only 85 % of the days. The radius covers the sampling error of the capability; a systematic shift of the operating conditions is absorbed by the margin that the cost-optimal commitment keeps below the capability limit.
6. **The integrated energy system provides the channel.** The gas engine and the absorption chiller supply 85 % of the energy drawn through the recovery channel ⟨; without the gas engine the firm capacity falls by … MW⟩, and computing flexibility and storage are complements: 3.0 MW and below 1 MW alone, 7.5 MW together.

For DR practice, these results suggest that consecutive-event contracts for AI data centers should be sized by the firm capacity of the controller that will operate them, not by physical capability, and that grid operators can accept larger firm commitments from data centers that coordinate computing and storage recovery across events. The study considers a single site in summer, two or three events per day and the Jiangsu settlement rules; the deferrable share and the lag bounds are assumptions informed by industrial programs. Future work will extend the framework to geographically distributed data centers that shift work between sites, to the thermal inertia of the server halls as a further recovery debt, and to the co-planning of batteries, connection capacity and DR contracts over the life of the data center.

## Appendix B. Proofs

**Notation and assumptions.** Let $\mathcal I=(e_A,s_B)$ be the interval of length $\tau$ between events A and B, and $\mathcal T_B$ the steps of event B with duration $D_B$. (H1) Without DR the grid import equals the baseline and the storage is idle in $\mathcal I\cup\mathcal T_B$. (H2) $\varepsilon_t$ and $\mathrm{COP}_t$ are constant in $\mathcal I\cup\mathcal T_B$; otherwise all bounds below hold with the largest $\varepsilon_t$ of the interval. Under (H1) the deviation of any schedule from the baseline is
$$P^{grid}_t-P^{base}_t=\varepsilon\sum_c\big(y_{c,t}-a_{c,t}\big)+P^{ch}_t-P^{dis}_t+\frac{Q^{TC}_t-Q^{TD}_t}{\mathrm{COP}}-\Delta g_t, \tag{B.1}$$
where the first term is catch-up ($>0$) or deferral ($<0$) of computing work and $0\le\Delta g_t\le g$ is the additional gas-engine output including its absorption-chiller equivalent.

**Quantities of Proposition 1.** $X_A=\varepsilon\sum_cB_c(e_A)$ is the computing debt left by A and $X_A^{due}$ its part that falls due before $e_B$; $E_s=\eta_d(E_A-\underline E)+\eta^T_d(S_A-\underline S)/\mathrm{COP}$ and $P_s=\bar P^{B}+\bar Q^{TD}/\mathrm{COP}$ are the dischargeable energy and power of the storage; $H=\sum_{t\in\mathcal I}[(P^{cap}-P^{base}_t)^++g]\Delta t$ is the recovery channel; $\bar X_B=\varepsilon\sum_c\bar B_c(e_B)$ is the deadline-limited virtual capacity at the end of B; $p_f=\varepsilon\sum_c\bar a_{c,B}\min(L_c,D_B)/D_B$ is the deferrable power that can still be paused in B; $W^{sw}$ is the work arriving in $\mathcal I$ with a deadline after $e_B$, which earliest-deadline-first execution can swap with due backlog; $\Gamma^0_B$ is the single-event capability. The power bound of Proposition 1 is
$$\Gamma^P=\max_{\substack{H_c+H_s\le H\\H_c\le\varepsilon G^{free}\tau,\ H_s\le\bar P^{B}\tau}}\Big\{p_f-\frac{(X_A^{due}-H_c-W^{sw})^+}{D_B}+\min\Big(P_s,\frac{E_s+\eta H_s}{D_B}\Big)\Big\}+g. \tag{B.2}$$

**Proof of Proposition 1.** (i) Restricting a feasible schedule of the two-event problem to event B gives a feasible schedule of the single-event problem with the same initial state, so $\Gamma_B\le\Gamma^0_B$.

(ii) Summing (B.1) over $\mathcal T_B$,
$$\Gamma_BD_B=\varepsilon\sum_c\big[B_c(e_B)-B_c(s_B^-)\big]+\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_B}\Big(P^{dis}_t-P^{ch}_t+\frac{Q^{TD}_t-Q^{TC}_t}{\mathrm{COP}}\Big)\Delta t+\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_B}\Delta g_t\Delta t .$$
By the deadline constraint (3) the first term is at most $\bar X_B-X(s_B^-)$, the second is at most the dischargeable energy $E_s(s_B^-)$ at the start of B, and the third is at most $gD_B$. In the interval, let $C_{\mathcal I}$ be the net catch-up energy and $D_{\mathcal I}$ the net energy drawn by the storage. Summing (B.1) over $\mathcal I$ with $P^{grid}_t\le P^{cap}$ gives the channel constraint $C_{\mathcal I}+D_{\mathcal I}\le H$. The states evolve as $X(s_B^-)=X_A-C_{\mathcal I}\ge0$ and $E_s(s_B^-)\le E_s+\phi(D_{\mathcal I})$ with $\phi(x)=\eta x$ for $x\ge0$ and $\phi(x)=x$ for $x<0$, because every MWh drawn for recharging returns only $\eta$ MWh in B. Hence
$$\Gamma_BD_B-gD_B\le\bar X_B-X_A+E_s+\max_{C+D\le H,\ C\le X_A}\{C+\phi(D)\}.$$
Catch-up has the marginal value 1 and recharge the marginal value $\eta<1$, so the maximum is attained by $C^*=\min(H,X_A)$ and $D^*=(H-X_A)^+$, whose value is $H^{eff}$. Dividing by $D_B$ gives $\Gamma^E$.

(iii) The computing reduction in B equals the backlog increase in B. Work arriving in B can be deferred by at most $\min(L_c,D_B)$, which contributes at most $p_fD_B$. The debt $X_A^{due}$ is either caught up in the interval ($H_c$), swapped with later-deadline work (at most $W^{sw}$), or executed in B, where it offsets the reduction one to one. The storage contribution is limited by $P_sD_B$ and by $E_s+\eta H_s$. Maximising over the allocation $H_c+H_s\le H$, subject to the free GPU and charging-power limits, gives (B.2). ∎

The bound is tight when the backlogs reach their deadline limits at the end of B, the storage is empty at the end of B and the channel is fully used in the order catch-up, then recharge. In the mechanism experiment (supplementary Fig. S1) the bound is within 0.05 MW of the optimal deliverable reduction in most cases; it is looser only for intervals below 0.5 h, where the per-step channel width and the charging power cannot be shifted in time as the aggregate $H$ assumes.

**Proof of Proposition 2.** Assume one deferrable class with constant arrival power $p_f$ and lag bound $L\ge D_B$, a debt $X\le p_fL$ at the start of the interval, dischargeable energy $\eta_dE_A$, a constant channel width $h\le\bar P^{B}$ ($H=h\tau$), full pausing of deferrable work in B and no gas engine. With catch-up $c\in[0,X]$ and recharge $s\ge0$ in the interval, $c+s\le H$, the deliverable reduction energy of B is
$$G(c,s)=\min\big(p_fD_B,\ p_fL-(X-c)\big)+\min\big(P_sD_B,\ \eta_dE_A+\eta s\big).$$
Let $c^*=[X-p_f(L-D_B)]^+$ be the necessary catch-up and $s^{req}=[P_sD_B-\eta_dE_A]^+/\eta$ the necessary recharge. The first term of $G$ is concave with slope 1 for $c<c^*$ and slope 0 afterwards; the second is concave with slope $\eta$ for $s<s^{req}$ and 0 afterwards. Maximising the sum of two separable concave functions under $c+s\le H$ allocates the channel greedily by marginal value: catch-up up to $c^*$, then recharge up to $s^{req}$. This is the joint recovery $c_{JR}=\min(H,c^*)$, $s_{JR}=(H-c^*)^+$. Independent recovery repays the whole debt first, $c_{IR}=\min(H,X)$, $s_{IR}=(H-X)^+$, and spends $X-c^*$ of the channel on catch-up of zero marginal value. Substituting both allocations,
$$G_{JR}-G_{IR}=\eta\Big[\min\big(s^{req},(H-c^*)^+\big)-\min\big(s^{req},(H-X)^+\big)\Big]\ge0, \tag{B.3}$$
with the maximum $\eta\min\{X,\,p_f(L-D_B),\,s^{req}\}$. The single-event capability $p_fD_B+P_sD_B$ is restored if and only if $c\ge c^*$ and $s\ge s^{req}$, which requires the channel energy $c^*+s^{req}$ under joint and $X+s^{req}$ under independent recovery. Dividing by $h$ and using $X-c^*=\min\{X,p_f(L-D_B)\}$ gives (14). The difference (B.3) vanishes if and only if $L\le D_B$ or $X=0$ (no debt can be carried), $s^{req}=0$ (B needs no storage), $H\le c^*$ (the channel only suffices for necessary catch-up) or $H\ge X+s^{req}$ (the channel repays both debts). ∎

Two consequences are used in Section 5. First, the minimum recovery energy $\tau^*h$ is fixed, so iso-capability curves are hyperbolas in the plane of the interval and the channel width: a longer interval and a larger connection margin substitute for each other, and joint recovery shifts the curve by the carried debt $\min\{X,p_f(L-D_B)\}$. Second, with several classes the necessary catch-up is $\sum_c[X_c-p_{f,c}(L_c-D_B)]^+$, so the long-deadline class C3 is carried across events first. The closed form is verified against a discrete-time LP on 68 cases ($p_f$ = 5 MW, $D_B$ = 1 h, $X$ = 5 MWh, $h$ = 3 MW, $L\in\{1,1.5,2,3\}$ h, $\tau\in[0,4]$ h) with a maximum deviation of $5\times10^{-15}$ MW; the interval reductions are 0, 0.83, 1.67 and 1.67 h, as given by (14) (supplementary Fig. S2).

**Proof of Proposition 3.** Write the empirical distribution as $N$ atoms of mass $1/N$ at $\xi_d$. Any $Q\in\mathcal B_\varepsilon(\hat P_N)$ is the image of a transport plan with average displacement at most $\varepsilon$. Moving mass between points on the same side of $C$, or upwards, does not reduce $Q(\xi\ge C)$, so a minimising plan only moves mass $w_d\in[0,1/N]$ from atoms $\xi_d\ge C$ to points arbitrarily close below $C$, at cost $w_d(\xi_d-C)$. The worst case is the fractional knapsack
$$\inf_{Q\in\mathcal B_\varepsilon}Q(\xi\ge C)=\frac{n(C)}N-\max\Big\{\textstyle\sum_dw_d:\ \sum_dw_d\,\delta_d(C)\le\varepsilon,\ 0\le w_d\le\frac1N\Big\},$$
whose optimum removes the atoms with the smallest margins first. The probability stays at least $\rho$ if and only if removing mass $r(C)/N$, i.e. the $\lfloor r\rfloor$ atoms with the smallest margins and the fraction $r-\lfloor r\rfloor$ of the next one, costs at least $\varepsilon$, which is (17). For $\varepsilon=0$ the condition reduces to $n(C)\ge\rho N$, the sample-average constraint. $\Phi_\rho(C)$ is non-increasing in $C$, because $n(C)$ and all margins decrease with $C$, so the feasible commitments form a lower set and $C^{DRO}_\rho(\varepsilon)$ is non-increasing in $\varepsilon$. When the atoms just above $C$ have large margins, $\Phi_\rho(C)$ is large and a moderate radius does not change the commitment. ∎

If the radius exceeds the finite-sample concentration radius $\varepsilon_{N,\beta}$ of the 1-Wasserstein distance [43], the true capability distribution lies in $\mathcal B_\varepsilon(\hat P_N)$ with confidence $1-\beta$ and the commitment $C^{DRO}_\rho$ is delivered in full with probability at least $\rho$. Such radii are conservative for $N=14$; the case study uses the expected estimation error (18a), which scales with the dispersion of the capability, and checks it by leave-one-out validation.

**Remark B.1 (single channel price).** For the joint model with fixed binaries, let $\lambda_t$ be the dual of the electric balance, $\nu_t$ that of the BESS energy equation and $\mu_{c,t}$ that of the backlog equation of class $c$. The KKT conditions give $\lambda_t=\pi_t+\lambda^{cap}_t+\lambda^{ev}_t$ when the import is interior, $\eta_c\nu_t=\lambda_t+c^{deg}$ for interior charging and $\mu_{c,t}=\varepsilon_t\lambda_t$ for interior execution. When catch-up and recharge are both interior, $\mu_{c,t}/\varepsilon_t=\lambda_t=\eta_c\nu_t-c^{deg}$: one channel price values both debts. Independent recovery schedules catch-up against $\pi_t$ or its own share, ignores $\lambda^{cap}_t$ and breaks this equality; IR-P restores the price part with a day-ahead signal but neither updates it with the realised state nor resolves the non-uniqueness of the IT response at that price (supplementary Fig. S6).
